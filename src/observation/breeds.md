# 🐕 Breeds — domestication and divergence

**What this is:** how the domestic dog branched from the wolf, and how humans shaped **breeds** — human-maintained populations selected for size, coat, and behaviour. A survey of the major breed
groups, a few familiar examples, and the health and temperament patterns that follow. Companion to [media/behavior.md](media/behavior.md).

---

## 🐺 From wolf to dog

The domestic dog (*Canis lupus familiaris*) descends from the grey wolf. Domestication began in the Late Pleistocene — roughly 15,000–30,000 years ago — and likely happened across more than one
region rather than in a single event. The usual picture is a **commensal** path: wolves that tolerated human camps got food scraps, and the bold-but-not-aggressive ones stayed. Selection then split
them from their wild relatives — not naturally tame, but **self-selected for tolerance**.

## 🧬 What a breed is

A **breed** is a population kept reproductively closed and bred to a written **standard** — a target for size, coat, colour, and behaviour. Registries (FCI, national kennel clubs) recognise
standards and sort breeds into groups. Breeds are recent and shallow: most were fixed in the last 200 years, and many pass through **genetic bottlenecks** when a few founders are used. That is why
pedigree animals can be highly uniform *and* highly inbred.

## 🩺 Health predispositions

Breed bottlenecks concentrate risk. Brachycephalic (flat-faced) breeds — Pug, Bulldog, Boston Terrier — struggle to breathe and cool down. Large breeds — German Shepherd, Labrador, Great Dane —
carry more **hip and elbow dysplasia**. Some lines have cardiac disease (Cavalier King Charles Spaniel), spinal disease (Dachshund), or skin and eye conditions. Mixed ancestry lowers the odds of a
single breed's recessive disorder, but does not remove it.

## 🧠 Temperament — nature and nurture

Behaviour has a **heritable** component — pointers point, herders herd — but it is not destiny. Puppy socialisation, training, and environment shape most of what a dog actually does. Breed predicts a
**statistical tendency**, not an individual's behaviour; two dogs of the same breed can differ sharply.

## 📌 Caveats

- **A breed is not an individual.** Standards describe averages; every dog varies.
- **Behaviour is multifactorial** — genes, early experience, training, and context.
- **Dog breeds are lineages built by artificial selection.** They are **not a valid model for human populations**:
  human genetic variation is mostly *within* groups and shifts gradually across geography, while human "race" is a social
  category, not a biological breed.
